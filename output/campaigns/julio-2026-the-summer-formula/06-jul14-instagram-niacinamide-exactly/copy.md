## SLIDE 2 — What it is

**Background:** Bone #F5F0E8

**Headline:** What it is.

**Body:**
Niacinamide is the amide form of vitamin B3 (niacin). It is water-soluble, stable across a wide pH range (3.5–7.5), and formulates well with most other actives — including retinoids, vitamin C, and AHAs.

INCI name: **Niacinamide**

It is distinct from niacin (nicotinic acid), which causes flushing. Niacinamide does not cause flushing. It is also distinct from nicotinamide riboside and NMN, which are oral supplements and function differently.

## SLIDE 3 — Mechanism: Barrier

**Background:** Lab Frost #ECF0EE

**Headline:** What it does: the barrier.

**Body:**
Niacinamide upregulates the synthesis of ceramides, fatty acids, and cholesterol in the stratum corneum — the three lipid classes that form the skin's barrier structure.

In practical terms: regular topical niacinamide use measurably increases ceramide content in the stratum corneum, reduces transepidermal water loss (TEWL), and improves overall barrier integrity.

This is why niacinamide is effective for dry, sensitive, and compromised barrier presentations — and why it performs particularly well in summer, when heat-induced TEWL is elevated.

## SLIDE 4 — Mechanism: Sebum

**Background:** Ink #1A1A18
**Text:** Bone #F5F0E8

**Headline:** What it does: sebum regulation.

**Body:**
Niacinamide reduces sebum excretion rate (SER) by inhibiting the activity of sebaceous glands. The mechanism involves modulation of triglyceride synthesis in sebocytes.

Clinical studies show a statistically significant reduction in SER at concentrations of 2–4% with consistent daily use over 4–8 weeks.

This is why niacinamide is commonly used in formulations for oily and acne-prone skin. It does not block pores. It reduces the rate at which sebum is produced. The result: smaller-looking pores, less congestion. Not the same mechanism as "shrinking" — but a meaningful functional outcome.

## SLIDE 5 — Mechanism: Anti-inflammatory

**Background:** Linen #F1EAE1

**Headline:** What it does: inflammation.

**Body:**
Niacinamide inhibits the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, specifically IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α. It also reduces the migration of inflammatory cells to skin.

This anti-inflammatory action is why niacinamide is effective in:
- Acne formulations (reduces inflammatory lesion count)
- Rosacea management (calms persistent redness)
- Post-UV exposure support (reduces UV-induced cytokine response)

The last application is particularly relevant in summer.

## SLIDE 6 — Mechanism: Hyperpigmentation

**Background:** Lab Mist #DAE9DF

**Headline:** What it does: hyperpigmentation.

**Body:**
Niacinamide inhibits the transfer of melanosomes from melanocytes to keratinocytes — the step in the pigmentation process where melanin moves from where it is produced to where it causes visible darkening in skin.

It does not inhibit melanin synthesis directly (that is tyrosinase inhibition — a different mechanism). It interrupts the transfer.

Clinical evidence supports efficacy for post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation and UV-induced pigmentation changes at concentrations of 4–10% with consistent use over 8–12 weeks. Results are visible but gradual.

## SLIDE 7 — Concentration and formulation

**Background:** Bone #F5F0E8

**Headline:** How to use it.

**Body:**
Effective concentration range: 2–10% in topical formulations.

At 2–4%: sebum regulation and anti-inflammatory effects dominant.
At 5%: clinical studies show barrier improvement and hyperpigmentation effects.
At 10%: used in targeted treatments for pigmentation and acne. Tolerance is good at this level for most skin types.

Above 10%: limited additional benefit. Some individuals report mild flushing at very high concentrations due to trace niacin impurities.

Niacinamide does not require an acidic pH to function. It performs at pH 3.5–7.5. It pairs well with vitamin C, retinol, and AHAs — the concern about niacinamide converting to niacin in acidic formulas is not supported by evidence at typical use concentrations and temperatures.

## SLIDE 8 — The takeaway

**Background:** Ink #1A1A18
**Text:** Bone #F5F0E8

**Headline:** The takeaway.

**Body:**
Niacinamide is not a pore-shrinker, a brightener, or a miracle active. It is a multifunctional B vitamin that does several specific, evidence-backed things simultaneously: supports ceramide synthesis, regulates sebum production, reduces pro-inflammatory cytokines, and interrupts melanin transfer.

It is one of the most well-tolerated actives in skincare. It works across multiple skin concerns without requiring complex formulation conditions. In summer specifically, its barrier-supporting and sebum-regulating effects address the two most common heat-related skin issues.

Concentration matters. Use it consistently.
