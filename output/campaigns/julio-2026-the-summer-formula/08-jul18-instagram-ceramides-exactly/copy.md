## SLIDE 2 — What they are

**Background:** Bone #F5F0E8

**Headline:** What they are.

**Body:**
Ceramides are a family of lipid molecules composed of a sphingosine base linked to a fatty acid chain. They are the dominant lipid class in the stratum corneum, accounting for approximately 50% of the lipid content by weight.

They are produced naturally by keratinocytes and secreted into the intercellular space, where they form the lamellar body structures that create the skin's lipid bilayer.

INCI names include: Ceramide NP, Ceramide AP, Ceramide EOP, Ceramide NS, Ceramide AS — among others. Different ceramide types have different structural roles.

## SLIDE 3 — How they function

**Background:** Lab Frost #ECF0EE

**Headline:** How they function.

**Body:**
The stratum corneum is often described by the "brick and mortar" model: corneocytes are the bricks; ceramides, cholesterol, and fatty acids are the mortar.

Ceramides provide structural cohesion. They form ordered lipid bilayers in the intercellular spaces that control:

- Water retention (preventing TEWL)
- Permeability to external agents
- Mechanical integrity of the surface barrier

Without adequate ceramide content, the lipid bilayer becomes disordered. The barrier becomes more permeable — to both water loss and to irritants and allergens entering the skin.

## SLIDE 4 — Why they deplete in summer

**Background:** Ink #1A1A18
**Text:** Bone #F5F0E8

**Headline:** What summer does to ceramide levels.

**Body:**
Several summer-specific conditions reduce ceramide content in the stratum corneum:

**UV radiation:** UV exposure induces ceramidase activity — an enzyme that degrades ceramides. Cumulative UV exposure measurably reduces ceramide levels over time.

**Surfactant exposure:** Increased sweating leads to increased face-washing and body-showering. Surfactants in cleansers extract ceramides from the lipid bilayer directly.

**Heat and humidity:** Elevated temperature increases enzymatic ceramide degradation rate. High humidity does not compensate for ceramide loss.

The result: compromised barrier function even in humid summer conditions.

## SLIDE 5 — Types of ceramides

**Background:** Lab Mist #DAE9DF

**Headline:** The ceramide family.

**Body:**
There are at least 12 ceramide subtypes in human skin, classified by fatty acid chain type and hydroxylation pattern.

The most commonly used in skincare formulations:

**Ceramide NP (2):** one of the most abundant in skin. Important for barrier integrity.
**Ceramide AP (6-II):** involved in the lamellar body structure.
**Ceramide EOP (1):** long-chain ceramide. Critical for the arrangement of the lipid bilayer.
**Ceramide NS (3):** water-binding properties in the intercellular space.

The best-performing ceramide formulations use multiple types simultaneously — because the lipid bilayer requires a combination, not a single ceramide type.

## SLIDE 6 — What topical ceramides actually do

**Background:** Bone #F5F0E8

**Headline:** What topical ceramides actually do.

**Body:**
There is an important distinction: topical ceramides do not integrate directly into the lipid bilayer the same way endogenous ceramides do.

What they do: act as a reservoir in the upper stratum corneum, from which ceramides can be incorporated into the intercellular lipid spaces over time. They also reduce TEWL directly through occlusive and semi-occlusive effects.

Clinical evidence shows measurable improvement in barrier function (TEWL reduction) and ceramide content in stratum corneum after consistent use of ceramide-containing formulations over 2–4 weeks.

The effect is real. The mechanism is partly indirect.

## SLIDE 7 — What to look for in formulations

**Background:** Lab Frost #ECF0EE

**Headline:** What to look for.

**Body:**
Effective ceramide formulations combine ceramides with cholesterol and fatty acids — the other two critical components of the intercellular lipid bilayer. The ratio matters: a 1:1:1 to 3:1:1 (ceramide:cholesterol:fatty acid) combination is most effective for barrier restoration.

Look for: multiple ceramide types (NP, AP, EOP), with cholesterol and fatty acids (stearic acid, linoleic acid) listed in the same formula.

CeraVe is the best-known example of this multi-ceramide + cholesterol approach. The patented MVE (MultiVesicular Emulsion) technology provides sustained ceramide release rather than a single-dose application.

## SLIDE 8 — The takeaway

**Background:** Ink #1A1A18
**Text:** Bone #F5F0E8

**Headline:** The takeaway.

**Body:**
Ceramides are not a moisturising ingredient in the traditional sense. They do not attract water or prevent evaporation through occlusion alone. They restore the architectural integrity of the stratum corneum — and that structural restoration is what reduces TEWL and improves hydration.

In summer, when UV exposure and increased cleansing actively degrade ceramide levels, topical ceramides are among the most evidence-backed interventions available. Not because they are trending. Because the mechanism is clear and the clinical data supports it.
