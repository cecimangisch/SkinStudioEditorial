## SLIDE 2 — What it is

**Background:** Lab Mist #DAE9DF

**Headline (DM Sans Bold):** What it is.

**Body (DM Sans Regular):**
Zinc oxide (ZnO) is an inorganic mineral compound. In sunscreen formulations, it functions as a UV filter — a substance that attenuates UV radiation before it reaches viable skin cells.

It is listed on INCI labels as: **Zinc Oxide**

It appears as a white powder in its raw form. In modern sunscreens, it is micronised or nano-sized to reduce visible whitening on skin. Both forms provide equivalent UV protection; only particle size differs.

---

## SLIDE 3 — How it works

**Background:** Bone #F5F0E8

**Headline (DM Sans Bold):** How it works.

**Body (DM Sans Regular):**
Zinc oxide works via two mechanisms simultaneously: it reflects UV radiation and it absorbs it.

The absorption mechanism is dominant. ZnO absorbs photons of UV energy and converts them into small amounts of heat — a process that occurs at the particle surface and does not generate the reactive intermediates that some organic filters do.

This is why zinc oxide is photostable. The conversion happens without degrading the molecule.

## SLIDE 4 — Spectrum coverage

**Background:** Ink #1A1A18
**Text:** Bone #F5F0E8

**Headline (DM Sans Bold):** What it covers.

**Body (DM Sans Regular):**
Zinc oxide is the only single UV filter approved in the EU and US that provides broad-spectrum coverage on its own.

UVB (290–320 nm): effective attenuation
UVA2 (320–340 nm): effective attenuation
UVA1 (340–400 nm): effective attenuation — including the deep UVA1 range where most chemical filters fall short

For context: avobenzone (a common UVA filter) covers UVA1 but is photounstable without stabilisers. Zinc oxide covers the same range and is inherently stable.

---

## SLIDE 5 — Stability

**Background:** Lab Frost #ECF0EE

**Headline (DM Sans Bold):** Why stability matters.

**Body (DM Sans Regular):**
A UV filter that degrades under sunlight provides less protection over time — even if you don't reapply.

Zinc oxide does not meaningfully degrade under UV exposure. Its crystal structure absorbs and dissipates energy without breaking down. This is what photostability means in practice.

By contrast, avobenzone degrades by approximately 50% after one hour of UV exposure without photostabilisers. Many sunscreen formulations pair avobenzone with octocrylene or Tinosorb S specifically to address this.

Zinc oxide does not require a stabiliser partner.

## SLIDE 6 — Skin tolerance

**Background:** Linen #F1EAE1

**Headline (DM Sans Bold):** Why sensitive skin tolerates it.

**Body (DM Sans Regular):**
Zinc oxide has a secondary anti-inflammatory effect. It moderates the activity of mast cells and inhibits certain pro-inflammatory cytokines. This is not incidental — it is why zinc oxide is used in wound-care formulations and why dermatologists recommend mineral sunscreens for rosacea, post-procedure skin, and acne-prone presentations.

It does not penetrate beyond the stratum corneum in standard particle sizes. No systemic absorption has been demonstrated at routine use concentrations.

---

## SLIDE 7 — Concentration in formulations

**Background:** Bone #F5F0E8

**Headline (DM Sans Bold):** Concentration in formulations.

**Body (DM Sans Regular):**
EU regulation permits zinc oxide as a UV filter at concentrations up to 25%.

In practice, most zinc oxide sunscreens use 15–22% to achieve SPF 30–50+.

Higher concentrations produce greater SPF but also greater whitening and heavier texture — the trade-off mineral sunscreen formulators work hardest to solve. Particle size reduction (micronised/nano ZnO) improves aesthetics without reducing efficacy.

Look for it: if a sunscreen claims mineral or physical and lists zinc oxide at or above 15%, the formulation is doing real work.

## SLIDE 8 — The takeaway

**Background:** Ink #1A1A18
**Text:** Bone #F5F0E8
**Fraunces spotlight:** "The takeaway"

**Headline (Fraunces):** The takeaway.

**Body (DM Sans Regular):**
Zinc oxide is not simply "safer" than chemical filters. That framing misses the point.

It is a photostable, broad-spectrum UV filter with a secondary anti-inflammatory effect and a strong safety record at standard concentrations. It is the only single filter that covers the full UV spectrum without additional stabilisers.

Its drawbacks are aesthetic, not functional: whitening, texture, difficulty blending into dark skin tones. Those are formulation problems, not safety trade-offs.

Understanding the difference matters.

## INSTAGRAM CAPTION

Zinc oxide covers UVA1, UVA2, and UVB on its own. No other single approved filter does that.


Zinc oxide is an inorganic UV filter that works by absorbing and reflecting UV radiation. Its crystal structure converts photon energy into heat without breaking down — which is what photostability means in practice. It does not degrade under UV exposure the way avobenzone does without stabilisation.

It covers the full UV spectrum: UVB, UVA2, and the deep UVA1 range (340–400nm) where most organic filters provide incomplete coverage. EU regulations permit it up to 25% in formulations. Most effective mineral SPFs use 15–22%.

Secondary benefit: genuine anti-inflammatory effect on skin. That is why it appears in wound care and in sunscreens formulated for rosacea and post-procedure use. Whitening on skin is an aesthetic issue, not a safety one — it is a particle size problem, and modern micronised ZnO largely addresses it.
